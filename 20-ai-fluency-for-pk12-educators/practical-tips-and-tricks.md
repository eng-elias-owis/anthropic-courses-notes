## 📌 Sub-Course 1: AI Fluency for pK-12 Educators

### Lesson 1: Welcome to AI Fluency for Educators

AI Fluency for educators means knowing when, how, and how much to use AI — while keeping your pedagogical expertise and values in the driver's seat. Your subject-matter knowledge, relationships with students, and professional judgment are what make AI outputs actually useful in a classroom context.

💡 Before starting the course, build a **context document**: What do you teach? What grade level? What are your goals this year? What concerns do you have about AI use with students? This document anchors every exercise in the course.

💡 Your pedagogical expertise is your greatest asset when working with AI. It's what you use to catch errors, adapt outputs, and judge whether something is truly appropriate for your students.

---

### Lesson 2: The 4D Framework

The 4D Framework operates in two loops:

- **Inner loop (Description ↔ Discernment):** your daily AI interactions — describe what you need, evaluate what comes back, refine.
- **Outer loop (Delegation ↔ Diligence):** higher-level decisions about when and how much to use AI in your practice.

For educators:
- **Delegation** — which tasks are right for AI? Lesson plan drafts ✅. Assessing individual student progress ❌. Writing feedback that knows a student's history ❌.
- **Description** — communicate precisely what you need: grade level, reading range, tone, format, learning objective.
- **Discernment** — evaluate AI output through your professional expertise before using it with students.
- **Diligence** — own what you put in front of students. If it came from AI, you verified it, adapted it, and stand behind it.

💡 Tag your context document by competency. Where does Delegation uncertainty cluster? That's your biggest opportunity for growth.

💡 The 4Ds are a loop, not a linear sequence. In practice, you'll flow between them fluidly — that's what AI fluency looks like in action.

### Lesson 3: Describing Your Context

The three elements of Description for educators:

1. **Audience** — who will read or use this output? (Grade level, reading range, family background, new teachers vs. experienced staff)
2. **Tone & style** — what voice fits? ("warm and encouraging, like my weekly newsletter" vs. "direct and data-focused, like my principal expects in meetings" vs. "playful and accessible for kindergartners")
3. **Format & structure** — what exactly do you need? A one-page handout with three sections? A 5-question exit ticket with answer key? A two-column comparison table?

💡 Tell AI who will read the output and what they need most — before describing the content itself. Audience context shapes everything.

💡 Don't describe format vaguely. "Format this as a 5-question exit ticket with an answer key at the bottom" is far more useful than "make it a quiz."

💡 If you want AI to match your voice, give it an example of your writing. One paragraph of your weekly newsletter is more useful than a paragraph of adjectives about your tone.

### Lesson 4: AI Capabilities and Limitations

Generative AI creates new content — text, images, code — rather than retrieving existing data. It's trained on massive text, refined to follow instructions, and can discuss almost any topic. But it also sounds confident when it's wrong.

**Current strengths:** versatility, conversational awareness, ability to differentiate content for multiple audiences in one response.

**Current limits:** knowledge cutoff dates, hallucinations (resources that don't exist), context window constraints, challenges with complex reasoning, no knowledge of your specific students.

💡 **Test the edges with your own content:** ask AI to explain a topic you know cold — at 2nd-grade, 7th-grade, and new-teacher levels in one response. Then ask it for 2–3 specific curriculum resources and verify at least one actually exists. Build your own gut check before relying on it in the classroom.

💡 AI has no knowledge of your specific students. It can generate differentiated content — but matching it to a particular student's needs, history, and learning profile is always your judgment call.

### Lesson 5: Creating High-Quality AI Outputs

The Description-Discernment loop is the core daily practice: describe → evaluate → refine → repeat. You describe precisely; you discern with your professional expertise; you feed that judgment forward as a sharper next prompt.

**What good Description looks like:**
- Specify grade level, reading range, subject area, and learning objective
- Name the audience explicitly: "This is for 7th graders with reading levels ranging from 3rd to 9th grade"
- Request a concrete format: "Give me this as a differentiated text in three versions: below grade, on grade, above grade"
- Include tone guidance: "Use accessible language, avoid jargon, keep sentences short"

**What good Discernment looks like:**
- Read for factual accuracy in your subject area
- Check that reading level adjustments actually landed
- Verify alignment with your learning objectives — not just the topic
- Judge whether your voice and classroom norms come through

💡 The first draft is raw material. Expect to iterate 2–3 times. The loop — describe, evaluate, refine — is the workflow, not a sign something went wrong.

💡 You'll spot errors AI can't — because you know the curriculum, the misconceptions your students carry, and what "grade-appropriate" means for *your* specific class.

### Lesson 6: Ethics & Responsible AI Use

The Delegation-Diligence loop is the ethics framework: decide upfront what's right to hand off (task, tool, data), and afterward take full responsibility for what you use.

**Non-negotiable data rules:**
- Never enter anything that identifies a student — no names, IDs, or personal details
- Anonymize or generalize before prompting when working with student performance data
- Use only district-approved AI tools for anything school-related
- Understand your tool's data handling policy before you start

**What stays human-led:**
- Assessments of individual students
- Communications with families about a specific child
- Disciplinary decisions
- Anything requiring knowledge of a student's history, IEP, or personal circumstances

💡 Before entering any data into an AI tool: ask "could this identify a student?" If yes, anonymize or don't use AI for it.

💡 Transparency with students and families matters. If AI helped produce something, be clear about its role — especially when it appears as feedback on student work.

### Lesson 7: AI's Constitution, Pedagogy, and You

Different AI models have different personalities and suit different tasks. Understanding this lets you choose the right tool for the right task. Your values are what keep you in the driver's seat regardless of which model you use.

**Build a personal AI value document** with three sections:
- **Intentions** — what are you trying to accomplish with AI in your practice?
- **Principles** — 2–3 rules you want to hold yourself to (e.g., "I always verify factual claims before using them with students")
- **Boundaries** — the one kind of work you'll always do yourself, no matter what

💡 Generic best practices don't substitute for your own values. Write the document in your voice, grounded in your actual context — then read it carefully and fix what doesn't sound like you.

💡 AI is not the pedagogy — you are. Your professional judgment, your relationships with students, and your values keep AI in its proper role as a tool, not a decision-maker.

### Lesson 8: Tying It All Together

A full 4D workflow applied to a real teaching task — what it looks like when an educator stays in the driver's seat:

1. **Delegation** — what does AI touch? What stays yours? Strip any student data before starting.
2. **Description** — write the full context: audience, learning objective, format, tone, constraints.
3. **Discernment** — what did AI get right? What was wrong, thin, or missing? What did you keep, change, or discard?
4. **Diligence** — would you put your name on this? Do you need to be transparent with anyone about how it was produced?

💡 Track what you added that only you could provide. That delta — your expertise, your knowledge of your students, your professional judgment — is what makes the output usable in your classroom.

💡 The 4Ds become invisible when they're used well. You're not following a framework anymore — you're just working.

---

### Lesson 9: Closure and Enrichment

The 4D Framework is a loop, not a line. You might flow from Delegation back to Description mid-task, or realize midway through Discernment that you need to re-delegate. That fluidity is the skill.

**Good first tasks to try:**
- A differentiated version of a reading or handout you already have
- A family communication draft in your voice
- An exit ticket or formative assessment for an upcoming lesson
- A planning outline for a unit you're starting

💡 Use blueprints (step-by-step guides for common educator tasks) at `claude.com/resources/use-cases` to guide your first attempts.

💡 Share your experience with colleagues. Your firsthand evidence — what AI does well and where you had to step in — is more useful to other educators than any abstract guidance.

## 📌 Sub-Course 2: AI Fluency for pK-12 Train the Trainer

### Lessons 10–13: The Train the Trainer Kit

This sub-course turns your own AI fluency outward — equipping you to lead an AI fluency workshop for your colleagues, in your voice. Complete the main educator course first; this kit assumes you've built your own fluency.

**The kit includes three ready-to-use pieces:**
- **The deck** — the full workshop, slide by slide, ready to present in-browser or download as PDF/HTML
- **The facilitator guide** — key points and activity setup for every slide, as speaker notes you toggle on while presenting
- **The learner workbook** — a hands-on companion for participants that covers the 4Ds, a delegation matrix, and a commitment to close; fillable and downloadable

**Three guardrails to open every session with:**
1. No prior knowledge needed — you don't need to be a tech expert to use AI well
2. Use your district's approved tool — check what's allowed before starting
3. Never enter anything that identifies a student — no names, IDs, or personal details, ever

💡 Personalize the "Meet your facilitator" slide before presenting — it saves in the browser.

💡 Have participants do the learner workbook alongside the session, not after. The exercises are designed to build on each other in real time.

💡 The warm-up exercise ("Have you ever...?") surfaces existing anxiety and experience without judgment. Let it breathe — this is where colleagues start to see each other as learners, not experts or skeptics.

## 💡 Key Principles Across the Path

- **Your pedagogical expertise is the asset** — AI generates; you evaluate, adapt, and own. The combination is what makes outputs classroom-ready.
- **Audience context before content** — always tell AI who will use the output and what they need. Grade level, reading range, and purpose shape everything.
- **Student data stays protected** — no names, no IDs, no personal details in any AI tool. This is non-negotiable.
- **Iteration is the workflow** — first drafts are raw material. The Description-Discernment loop is the practice, not a sign of failure.
- **You are the pedagogy** — the 4D Framework keeps AI in its proper role: a tool that accelerates your work, not one that replaces your judgment about students, learning, or values.
