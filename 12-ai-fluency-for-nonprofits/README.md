## 📚 Course Overview

This course is designed for **nonprofit professionals** who want to use AI effectively, ethically, and in ways that advance their mission. It addresses the gap between AI enthusiasm and meaningful implementation in the nonprofit sector by providing practical, hands-on training grounded in the **AI Fluency Framework** (the 4Ds).

The course acknowledges a core tension in the sector: nonprofits are often excited about AI's potential but unsure how to implement it in ways that are **effective, efficient, ethical, and safe**. It provides a structured approach for navigating this tension — emphasizing that **mission-centered thinking should guide all AI use** in the nonprofit context.

### What Makes This Course Different

Unlike generic AI training, this course is **sector-specific**:
- Every example, exercise, and scenario is grounded in nonprofit realities (grant proposals, donor communications, program data, beneficiary privacy, volunteer coordination)
- It addresses the **unique constraints** nonprofits face: limited budgets, small teams, sensitive data, accountability to funders and communities
- It emphasizes that every AI efficiency gain should ultimately translate to **greater impact for the communities you serve**
- It treats AI Fluency as an ongoing practice, not a one-time training

### The Mission Lens

A recurring theme: **AI should serve your mission, not the other way around.** This means:
- Using AI to free up time for higher-impact, human-centered activities
- Being intentional about *which* tasks you delegate to AI
- Maintaining dignity and respect when working with vulnerable populations
- Knowing when to choose *not* to use AI, even if it would be more efficient

## 🎯 Key Concepts

### The 4Ds — AI Fluency Competencies

| Competency | What It Covers |
|---|---|
| **Delegation** | Deciding what work should be done by humans vs. AI; problem awareness, platform awareness, and task distribution |
| **Description** | Communicating effectively with AI; defining products, processes, and performance |
| **Discernment** | Critically evaluating AI outputs; assessing quality, methodology, and behavior |
| **Diligence** | Taking responsibility for AI use; creation, transparency, and deployment diligence |

### The Two Loops

The 4Ds are organized into **two nested loops**:
- **Delegation-Diligence Loop** — the *strategic/ethical* level: deciding when to use AI, taking responsibility for outcomes
- **Description-Discernment Loop** — the *tactical/craft* level: day-to-day prompting and evaluating outputs

### Sub-Components of Each Competency

| Competency | Sub-Components |
|---|---|
| **Delegation** | Problem Awareness (understanding your goals), Platform Awareness (knowing your tools' capabilities and limitations), Task Delegation (distributing work thoughtfully) |
| **Diligence** | Creation Diligence (being intentional about tool choice), Transparency Diligence (honesty about AI's role), Deployment Diligence (verifying and vouching for outputs) |
| **Description** | Product Description (defining outputs), Process Description (guiding the approach), Performance Description (shaping AI behavior during collaboration) |
| **Discernment** | Product Discernment (evaluating quality), Process Discernment (evaluating how AI arrived at outputs), Performance Discernment (evaluating AI behavior) |

### Data Hygiene Principles

A core theme for nonprofits handling sensitive data:
- **Match the tool to the task** — paid accounts with strict data retention policies are not the same as free tools that may train on your data
- **Apply Problem Awareness and Platform Awareness before starting** — often you can get full benefit from AI without sharing sensitive information
- **Work backwards from your goal** — determine what data is truly necessary; remove identifying information when not needed
- **You have options if something goes wrong** — delete conversations, request data deletion, follow organizational protocols

### Being "The Human in the Loop"

For mission-driven organizations, being the human in the loop means:
- **You decide** what problems AI should help solve
- **You evaluate** whether solutions align with your values
- **You maintain** the relationships and real-world impact that define nonprofit work
- **You avoid dependency** through understanding, not avoidance — regularly ask "can we explain what the AI is doing?"

## 📖 Lesson Summaries

### Lesson 1: Welcome to AI Fluency for Nonprofits
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Define what AI Fluency means; articulate your values and constraints; identify your objectives for integrating AI tools

This introductory lesson acknowledges the gap between AI enthusiasm and meaningful implementation in the nonprofit sector. It introduces AI Fluency as the ability to work with AI systems in ways that are **effective, efficient, ethical, and safe** — a combination of practical skills, knowledge, insights, and values.

**Key Takeaways:**
- AI Fluency is a combination of practical skills, knowledge, insights, and **values** that help you adapt to evolving AI technologies
- The 4D Framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) forms the foundation of the course
- **Mission-centered thinking** should guide all nonprofit AI use
- Every AI efficiency gain should ultimately translate to **greater impact for the communities you serve**

**Practical Exercise — Defining Your Organizational Context:**
- **Part I (Self-Reflection):** Answer key questions about your mission, role, constraints, and values
- **Part II (Collaboration with AI):** Use Claude to interview you and build a reusable context document
- Use the prompt: *"If AI could help me _______, I would be able to spend more time _______."*

**Practical Tips:**
- Create a **reusable context document** with your organization's mission, values, role, and goals
- Share this document at the start of new AI conversations to establish shared understanding
- Focus on how AI efficiency could free you up for higher-impact, human-centered activities
- Don't share everything at once — make it conversational and refine as you go

### Lesson 2: The 4D Framework
**Estimated time:** 30 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Explain how the 4D Framework applies to nonprofit work; define each of the 4Ds

This lesson introduces the four interconnected competencies that form the foundation of AI Fluency, with nonprofit-specific examples for each sub-component. It emphasizes that the 4Ds are not sequential steps but a **continuously interacting system**.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Delegation-Diligence loop** guides higher-level decisions about when to use AI
- **Description-Discernment loop** guides effective day-to-day interactions
- Each time you interact with AI, you are (or should be) interacting with all four competencies in an interconnected way
- Mapping your questions and concerns to the 4Ds reveals where you're focusing — and where you might be avoiding a harder competency

**Practical Exercise — Mapping Your AI Needs:**
- Write a list of your questions and concerns about using AI
- Ask AI: *"Can you help me understand where each of these questions and concerns fits within the AI Fluency 4D framework?"*
- Reflect: Which competency dominated? Is this where you most need to develop, or are you avoiding a harder area?

**Stretch Goal — Identify Cross-Competency Challenges:**
- Recognize that some problems require developing multiple competencies simultaneously
- Example: "I need both Delegation skills (deciding if AI should help with volunteer communications) AND Diligence skills (ensuring we protect volunteer privacy)"

**Practical Tips:**
- The pattern of your questions reveals your current focus — be honest about it
- Some competencies enable others: Discernment concerns about accuracy won't matter until you develop Description skills
- Don't try to develop all four at once — identify your priority and start there

### Lesson 3: Researching with AI
**Estimated time:** 50 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Use Description to craft effective prompts; use Discernment to evaluate AI-generated research

This lesson demonstrates the Description-Discernment loop in action through a research scenario: Maria, an executive director expanding her housing nonprofit from Portland to Seattle, uses AI to research policy landscapes, funding opportunities, and compliance requirements.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Effective Description provides context** — explain who you are, who you serve, and what you specifically need to know
- **Discernment isn't optional** — flag specific claims for verification, notice gaps, question recency
- **The loop is iterative** — your first prompt rarely gives you everything; use each response to craft better follow-ups
- **AI accelerates research but doesn't replace expertise** — you're still the decision-maker

**Exercise 1 — Policy and Legislation Tracking:**
- Choose a policy area relevant to your work
- Craft a prompt with: specific policy, nonprofit context, what you need to know, time/geo scope
- Apply Discernment: identify claims needing verification, missing perspectives, outdated info
- Stretch: ask AI to track the original source for one key claim

**Exercise 2 — Donor or Grant Prospecting:**
- Choose your focus: grant opportunities, corporate donors, or foundation prospects
- Craft a prompt with: mission, program details, funding parameters, what makes a "good fit" beyond topic alignment
- Apply Discernment: verify funders actually fund orgs of your size; check deadlines; note values alignment

**Practical Tips:**
- Build verification habits into your workflow — never trust AI research without checking
- Generic prompts get generic results; context-rich prompts get actionable insights
- AI often misses what only you or your network would know — factor in that gap

### Lesson 4: Writing with AI
**Estimated time:** 50 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Apply Description to craft context-rich prompts for writing; apply Discernment to evaluate outputs

This lesson applies the Description-Discernment loop to writing, with a focus on maintaining **authenticity and human connection**. It follows James, who runs a small environmental justice organization, as he uses AI to write an emergency grant proposal under a 48-hour deadline.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Upload past successful work** to help AI understand your organization's voice and approach
- **Inject the details AI can't know** — track record, partnerships, staff expertise, community relationships
- **Be specific in revisions** — instead of "make this more compelling," say "add these specific details"
- **Own the final result** — when you submit work created with AI assistance, you should be able to stand behind every word

**Exercise 1 — Grant Proposal:**
- Select a program needing funding
- Craft a prompt with: grant opportunity, program details, organizational voice, requirements, what makes your approach unique
- Apply Discernment: verify all data; check if language reflects your actual voice; assess framing of beneficiaries

**Exercise 2 — Social Media Posts:**
- Choose your content goal (impact, event, education, thanks)
- Craft a prompt with: platform/format, audience, key message, voice, visual needs, constraints
- Apply Discernment: check tone match; verify claims; assess dignity of language; evaluate accessibility

**Practical Tips:**
- The most common mistake is generic prompts — invest time in context
- Watch for **deficit-based framing** or problematic language about beneficiaries
- AI helps you write faster, but **you shape the substance**
- Build a **cognitive environment** through back-and-forth that aligns the tool to your needs over time

### Lesson 5: Understanding Privacy and Data
**Estimated time:** 30 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Articulate privacy concerns; evaluate AI tools based on data handling; practice data hygiene

This lesson addresses one of the most common concerns nonprofit professionals have: **data privacy**. It covers what actually happens to data you share with AI tools, how to evaluate platforms, and how to prepare sensitive data for safe use.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **AI introduces new privacy considerations** — particularly around training: some tools use your inputs to train future models
- **Different tools have different rules** — the free AI you use to brainstorm event themes is not the same as a paid account with strict data retention policies
- **Safe AI use isn't about avoiding it** — it's about using it responsibly
- **You can often remove identifying information entirely** — for pattern analysis, you likely don't need names, contact details, or PII

**A Note on Claude's Privacy Settings:**
- Detailed data policy at privacy.anthropic.com and trust.anthropic.com
- You can adjust settings directly in the Claude app, including opting out of training on your conversations
- Other providers should have similar resources — if they don't, that's a red flag

**Exercise 1 — Evaluating Data Sensitivity:**
- Review a sample dataset (donor history, survey responses, grant report)
- Identify PII fields, essential information, removable info, worst-case exposure scenario
- Plan: what to remove, what tool tier to use, what verification steps

**Exercise 2 — Practicing Data Hygiene:**
- Select a real document with sensitive info
- Sanitize it: replace names with generic identifiers, generalize locations, strip contact info
- Test with AI: did removing identifying info limit AI's ability to help?

**Practical Tips:**
- Think about AI privacy the same way you think about email, cloud storage, and CRMs
- Break tasks into component parts — often you can get full benefit without sharing sensitive info
- If something goes wrong: delete the conversation, request data deletion, follow organizational protocols
- Match your tool to your task — tools with more protection allow safer sharing of sensitive data

### Lesson 6: Data Analysis with AI
**Estimated time:** 50 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Use the Delegation-Diligence loop to validate AI's analytical capabilities; apply Description and Discernment to identify patterns

This lesson addresses a critical question: **How do I know I can trust the results?** It follows Rio, a program director at Valley Veterans Services, as he systematically validates AI's analytical capabilities using past data where he already knows the answers.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Test AI against data you already understand** — validate using past data where you know correct results
- **Use Discernment to identify gaps** in AI's reasoning — note what additional Description you need
- **Build validated approaches, not blind trust** — document what works so you can replicate it
- **AI can help even if you're not data-savvy** — it can brainstorm solutions, write Excel formulas, reformat messy data

**Exercise 1 — Messaging Analysis:**
- Gather 10-20 examples of your organization's communications (mix of high and low performing)
- Ask AI to identify patterns in themes, language, tone, formatting
- Apply Discernment: do patterns match your intuition? What's AI missing?
- Stretch: audit messaging against stated mission and values

**Exercise 2 — Analyzing Donor Giving Patterns:**
- Use the sanitized donor dataset from Lesson 5
- Ask AI to identify patterns in: retention rates, recurring vs. one-time, campaign effectiveness, giving trends
- Apply Discernment: do trends match what you know? Is AI missing relationship factors?
- **Critical question:** What might the costs of efficiency recommendations be? (e.g., focusing on major donors at expense of small donors)

**Exercise 3 (Stretch) — Trend Analysis for Community Needs:**
- Combine multiple data sources: program data, external reports, news, policy
- Ask AI to identify emerging patterns, demand trends, service gaps
- Apply rigorous Discernment: how do AI's predictions compare with your direct community experience?

**Practical Tips:**
- **Validation builds confidence but doesn't eliminate responsibility** — you're still accountable
- Consider systemic factors and local context that AI cannot see
- When AI suggests efficiency recommendations, always ask: *what's the cost?*
- Keep asking for clarifications so you understand the process, not just the output

### Lesson 7: Workflow Augmentation
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Apply all four dimensions of the 4D Framework together to build a repeatable procedure with AI

This lesson brings together all four 4Ds to build a practical workflow automation. It follows Emily, a development coordinator preparing for her organization's annual gala, as she sets up an AI-assisted email response system.

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Start with Problem Awareness** — analyze your actual workload before touching any AI tools
- **Task Delegation means asking "should AI do this?"** not just "can AI do this?"
- **Test iteratively with real examples** — you'll discover gaps in your descriptions that need refinement
- **Practice all three types of Diligence** — Creation, Deployment, and Transparency

**Exercise 1 — Mapping Automation Opportunities:**
- Audit your past week: list 5-10 repetitive tasks
- For each: note frequency, duration, standardization level
- Categorize: AI can handle / AI can assist (human decides) / Human should handle

**Exercise 2 — Building Your Automation Description:**
- **Product Description:** What inputs? What outputs?
- **Process Description:** What are the steps? Decision points? Escalation triggers?
- **Performance Description:** What tone? How to handle uncertainty? What it should never do?
- **Test with 3-5 real examples** from your work

**Exercise 3 (Stretch) — Planning for Diligence:**
- **Creation Diligence:** Why is this appropriate for AI? What could go wrong?
- **Deployment Diligence:** Review every output or sample? What triggers pause?
- **Transparency Diligence:** Who needs to know? How to disclose? Follow-up options?

**Practical Tips:**
- Categorization criteria matter: standardized responses are good candidates; emotional situations need humans
- Describe the system precisely enough for AI to execute it — vague descriptions get vague results
- Plan for the human handoff — always provide a path to human attention
- Sample monitoring is fine for established systems, but review every output early on

### Lesson 8: Integration
**Estimated time:** 45 minutes  
**Learning Objectives:** Integrate AI into your organization in ways that strengthen human capacity; address dependency concerns; create an organizational AI policy

This lesson addresses two of the most common concerns about organizational AI adoption: **How do I avoid becoming too dependent on AI?** and **How do we balance efficiency with the human touch?**

**Key Takeaways:**
- **Being "the human in the loop"** means more than oversight — it means ensuring AI serves your mission
- **Avoid dependency through understanding, not avoidance** — regularly ask "can we explain what the AI is doing?"
- **AI should free you for more human work, not less** — at its best, AI cuts through noise so you can focus on handwritten cards, personalized outreach, time with individuals
- **Set cultural norms around productivity early** — discuss what happens with time saved
- **An AI policy helps scale your understanding** across the organization

**Exercise — Draft Your Organizational AI Policy:**

The exercise walks through **seven sections** to compile:
1. **Platform awareness** — which tools, which are prohibited, data retention policies
2. **Task delegation** — what's appropriate for AI, what stays human, who decides
3. **Expectations and capacity** — how time saved is redirected, realistic role expectations
4. **Quality and oversight** — who reviews outputs, verification steps, mistake handling
5. **Transparency** — what stakeholders need to know, AI attribution approach
6. **Values alignment** — how to ensure AI serves mission, dignity in AI-assisted work
7. **Compile and review** — work with AI to synthesize into a usable policy document

**Practical Tips:**
- When you can't explain what the AI is doing, **rework your processes** until you can
- Be explicit about when to choose *not* to use AI, even if it would be more efficient
- Build AI capacity across the team — don't create a single "AI expert" dependency
- Plan how you'll introduce and revisit the policy as your organization's AI use evolves

## 💡 Top Practical Takeaways

1. **Start with a reusable context document.** Before you use AI for anything substantive, work with Claude to build a document capturing your organization's mission, your role, constraints, values, and goals. Share it at the start of new AI conversations to establish shared understanding immediately.

2. **Mission-centered thinking guides all AI use.** Every AI efficiency gain should ultimately translate to greater impact for the communities you serve. If a use case doesn't serve your mission, it doesn't serve your organization — even if it saves time.

3. **Ask "should AI do this?" not just "can AI do this?"** Task Delegation is about judgment, not capability. Standardized responses are good candidates; emotional situations and high-stakes decisions need humans. Some tasks should stay fully human by design.

4. **Test AI against data you already understand.** Before trusting AI with new analysis, validate it using past data where you know the correct results. Document what works so you can replicate it. Validation builds confidence but doesn't eliminate responsibility.

5. **Effective Description provides rich context.** Generic prompts get generic results. Explain who you are, who you serve, what you specifically need to know, and what makes your approach unique. Upload past successful work to help AI understand your voice.

6. **Discernment isn't optional — it's a practice.** Flag specific claims for verification, notice gaps, question recency of information. Apply Discernment to every AI output, especially for high-stakes content like grant proposals and donor communications.

7. **Match the tool to the task.** Free AI tools may train on your data; paid accounts often have strict data retention policies. For sensitive work — donor information, beneficiary details, internal data — use tools with appropriate protections, or sanitize data before sharing.

8. **Work backwards from your goal to determine necessary data.** For pattern analysis, you usually don't need names, contact details, or PII. Replace with generic identifiers, generalize locations, strip contact info. You can often get full benefit from AI without sharing sensitive information.

9. **Be specific in your revisions.** Instead of "make this more compelling," say "add these specific details about our community relationships." Vague feedback gets vague results. You shape the substance; AI helps you write faster.

10. **Avoid dependency through understanding, not avoidance.** Regularly reflect on your processes and ask "can we explain what the AI is doing?" If yes, that's healthy augmentation. If not, rework your processes until you can. AI should free you for more human work, not less.

### Quick Reference: When to Use AI (and When Not To)

| AI is Good For | Keep Human |
|---|---|
| Standardized responses to common questions | High-stakes decisions |
| Documented information and processes | Emotional or sensitive situations |
| Drafting, formatting, brainstorming | Complex judgment calls |
| Pattern analysis (with sanitized data) | Anything requiring deep relationship context |
| Writing assistance for time-pressed deadlines | First-draft creative or strategic work |
| Research acceleration and orientation | Final accountability for sensitive outputs |
