## 📚 Course Overview

This course is designed for **educators who have already been introduced to the AI Fluency Framework** and are ready to teach it to their students. It covers strategies for teaching the four core competencies (the "4Ds": **Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence**), how to assess AI Fluency, how to design AI-integrated assignments, and how to apply the framework within your specific discipline.

The AI Fluency Framework operates on **two levels**:
1. **Descriptive** — explains what happens when people work with AI
2. **Normative** — guides people toward better AI collaboration practices

Understanding both levels is essential when teaching the framework to others.

---

## 🎯 Key Concepts

### The 4Ds — AI Fluency Competencies
| Competency | What It Covers |
|---|---|
| **Delegation** | Strategic decisions about whether, when, and how to involve AI; goal-setting and task breakdown |
| **Description** | Prompting and conversation quality; building shared context with AI over time |
| **Discernment** | Critically evaluating AI outputs; recognizing quality vs. mediocre results |
| **Diligence** | Ethical decision-making; transparency, accountability, and responsible AI use |

### The Two Loops
The 4Ds are organized into **two nested loops**:
- **Delegation-Diligence Loop** — the *strategic/ethical* level: big-picture decisions about AI use, accountability, and responsible design
- **Description-Discernment Loop** — the *tactical/craft* level: moment-to-moment prompting, context-building, and evaluating outputs

The loops **work together**: Delegation-Diligence sets the strategic container; Description-Discernment fills it with iterative interaction.

### Four Teaching Approaches
| Approach | Best For |
|---|---|
| **Linear** | Beginners needing structured, sequential skill-building (Delegation → Description → Discernment → Diligence) |
| **Non-linear** | Experienced students who need flexibility to match real-world workflows |
| **Focused** | Deep dives into a single D when time is limited or one competency is most critical |
| **Loop-based** | Students ready to see AI collaboration as nested, iterative strategic and tactical processes |

## 📖 Lesson Summaries

### Lesson 1: Welcome & Approaches to Teaching AI Fluency
**Learning Objectives:** Select and adapt teaching approaches; design loop-based or linear learning experiences

This introductory lesson welcomes educators already familiar with the AI Fluency Framework and introduces the four teaching approaches. It emphasizes that the framework is *both descriptive and normative* — students need to understand not just how AI interactions work, but how to improve them.

**Key Takeaways:**
- The AI Fluency Framework serves as both a model and a guide
- Your choice of teaching approach should match your students' readiness, available time, and learning goals
- Linear approaches work best for beginners; loop-based and non-linear approaches suit experienced students
- Create a **teaching context document** (with Claude's help) that captures your students' backgrounds, constraints, and goals — reuse it to maintain continuity across lessons

**Practical Tips:**
- Before designing your curriculum, map your teaching context: student AI experience level, institutional constraints, time available
- Use Claude (or any preferred AI) to help synthesize your teaching context into a reusable document
- Explore all four approaches and see how each would work for your specific student cohort before committing to one
- The framework works with *any* AI assistant — not just Claude; adapt examples to what your students actually use

---

### Lesson 2: The Delegation-Diligence Loop
**Learning Objectives:** Help students leverage the Delegation-Diligence loop for responsible design and decision-making

This lesson explores the *strategic and ethical* loop of the framework. Delegation (choosing what to hand off to AI) and Diligence (ensuring accountability and ethical use) are not separate steps — they continuously inform each other. Delegation decisions raise ethical questions; ethical constraints reshape delegation strategies.

**Key Takeaways:**
- This loop addresses the question: *"How do I make good decisions about AI?"* — not just *"How do I use AI?"*
- The loop runs **both ways**: Delegation → Diligence AND Diligence → Delegation
- Students who master this loop can articulate *why* their AI collaboration choices align with their goals and values
- Constraints (ethical, transparency, accountability) are **creative catalysts**, not limitations

**Practical Tips:**
- Create **realistic discipline-specific scenarios** with concrete decision points where students must navigate between strategy and ethics
- Design a **worksheet with structured prompts** guiding students from problem awareness through platform selection to ethical implications
- Build in a "reverse flow" exercise: start with ethical constraints and show how they *clarify and improve* delegation choices
- Use step-by-step guides rather than open-ended prompts — students learn the loop by walking through it explicitly

### Lesson 3: The Description-Discernment Loop
**Learning Objectives:** Help students build cognitive environments and move beyond single prompts to iterative AI conversations

This lesson focuses on the *tactical/craft* level of AI collaboration. Description (building rich context, vocabulary, and interaction patterns) and Discernment (evaluating outputs and adjusting) work together to create **cognitive environments** — shared spaces of understanding between human and AI.

The lesson introduces **three lenses** for understanding collaboration:
| Lens | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Product** | What we're creating — evolves through iteration |
| **Process** | How we approach the work — shared problem-solving methods |
| **Performance** | How we interact — communication styles and AI behaviors |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Students often arrive expecting "prompt tricks" — they need to develop a more sophisticated view of AI as a collaborative partner
- The goal is moving from **automation** (AI does the task) to **augmentation** (human + AI do more together)
- Successful cognitive environments include shared vocabulary, established interaction patterns, and mechanisms for building on previous exchanges
- Discernment improves Description: evaluating outputs teaches you to describe better next time

**Practical Tips:**
- Design assignments requiring **multiple interactions over time**, not just one-shot prompts
- Share your *own* AI collaboration process with students — show the messy iterative reality
- Have students document the **evolution** of their AI conversations — how their understanding of the product, process, and relationship changed
- Teach the Product/Process/Performance framework explicitly so students can diagnose *why* a collaboration isn't working

### Lesson 4: How Do We Assess the 4Ds?
**Learning Objectives:** Apply outcome, process, and reflection-based assessment strategies; create rubrics for the 4D competencies

Assessment of AI Fluency requires three complementary approaches:
| Approach | Focus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome-based** | What students produce through AI collaboration | Improved AI outputs, comparative analysis |
| **Process-based** | How students work with AI over time | Annotated chat logs, recorded narrations |
| **Reflection-based** | Metacognitive awareness about AI use | Learning journals, personal policy statements |

**Assessment by D:**
- **Delegation:** Assess goal-setting quality, task breakdown, tool selection rationale
- **Description:** Evaluate conversation quality and iterative refinement
- **Discernment:** Look at critical evaluation skills and quality judgment
- **Diligence:** Examine ethical decision-making and accountability practices

**Key Takeaways:**
- All three assessment approaches together give the most complete picture
- **Observable actions and concrete artifacts** are more reliable than assumptions about understanding
- Assessment should be a **learning opportunity**, not just measurement
- Use three performance levels (e.g., emerging/developing/proficient) with discipline-specific, concrete language

**Practical Tips:**
- For each D you assess, decide explicitly: outcome measures, process measures, reflection measures — or a combination
- Co-create assessment language with Claude: transform abstract concepts like "good understanding" into specific observable behaviors
- Plan what to assess directly vs. what students can document themselves — not everything needs direct grading
- Build rubrics that use **your course's specific language**, not generic AI terms

### Lesson 5: Designing Assignments for AI Fluency
**Learning Objectives:** Design assignments that develop and demonstrate AI Fluency; manage increased volume of AI-enhanced work

Three principles for effective AI Fluency assignments:
1. **Authenticity** — mirror real-world AI collaboration in your field
2. **Iteration** — build in refinement opportunities that showcase growth
3. **Pedagogical Transparency** — be clear you're assessing the *collaboration process*, not just outputs

**Assignment Types:**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Outcome-based** | Improving AI outputs, comparing AI systems, producing AI-enhanced work |
| **Process-based** | Annotated chat logs, recorded narrations of AI interaction, workflow documentation |
| **Reflection-based** | Learning journals, personal AI policy statements, metacognitive essays |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Effective assignments combine 2-3 components that work together coherently
- Build in genuine problems where AI partnership adds real value — not artificial tasks
- Manage increased workload strategically: detailed rubrics, peer review, lightning-round conferences, selective sampling

**Practical Tips:**
- Use **annotated chat logs** — students highlight key decision points and explain why they made them (process-based + reflection)
- Create **personal AI policy statements** where students articulate their own rules for AI use in their field
- Use **peer review** for process components — reduces grading burden and builds evaluation skills
- Try **selective sampling**: don't grade every artifact; have students flag their best examples, then spot-check others
- Hold **5-minute lightning conferences** instead of written feedback for process components

### Lesson 6: AI's Impact and Your Discipline
**Learning Objectives:** Analyze AI's specific impacts on curriculum, pedagogy, and assessment in your field; create a discipline-specific position paper

AI disrupts disciplines differently. Three key questions to analyze impact in your field:
1. **What gets automated?** — Which routine tasks in your field is AI handling or will handle?
2. **Where does human-AI partnership add the most value?** — What unique human+AI synergies exist?
3. **How do we prepare students for accountability?** — How will future professionals manage and remain responsible for AI systems?

**Three Areas of Disruption:**
| Area | What May Change |
|---|---|
| **Curriculum** | Which foundational concepts matter more or less as AI handles routine work |
| **Pedagogy** | New possibilities (personalized tutoring, instant feedback) and risks (short-circuiting genuine learning) |
| **Assessment** | What "genuine understanding" means when students have AI access |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Disruption isn't uniform — curriculum might stay stable while assessment transforms, or vice versa
- Some disruptions are **opportunities** (leverage them); others are **problems** (solve them)
- AI Fluency **amplifies** disciplinary expertise — it doesn't replace it
- Your expertise determines which disruptions matter and how to respond

**Practical Tips:**
- Prepare compelling, **discipline-specific answers** to "Why learn X when AI can do it?" — generic answers don't land
- Identify which human skills become *more* crucial as AI handles routine work (judgment, synthesis, creativity, ethics)
- Develop assessments that can't be shortcut by AI — focus on process, growth, and synthesis
- Write a **position paper** articulating your own stance on AI in your discipline — this becomes a resource for students and colleagues

### Lesson 7: Applying Discipline Expertise to AI Fluency
**Learning Objectives:** Apply disciplinary expertise to create field-specific 4D applications; collaborate with colleagues on shared frameworks

This final content lesson demonstrates how to make the 4Ds concrete for your specific field by **making tacit knowledge explicit**. The deeper students understand *quality, methods, and ethics* in your discipline, the better they can direct AI.

**Discipline-Specific Discussion Questions (with Colleagues):**
| D | Key Question |
|---|---|
| **Discernment** | *"What does quality look like in our field?"* — what distinguishes outstanding from mediocre work? |
| **Description** | *"How do we communicate in our discipline?"* — specific products, expert thought processes, professional norms |
| **Delegation** | *"What work happens in our field?"* — which elements require human judgment vs. AI assistance? |
| **Diligence** | *"What are our field's values and standards?"* — ethical frameworks, transparency norms, accountability |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Making tacit disciplinary knowledge explicit (best practices, ethics, research genres) prepares students for effective AI collaboration
- Collaborative work with colleagues builds **shared frameworks** stronger than any individual effort
- Students who understand quality, methods, and ethics can better *direct* AI — they become irreplaceable
- The goal: prepare students to develop uniquely human capabilities that complement AI

**Practical Tips:**
- Schedule structured time with colleagues to work through the 4D framework together — this is the most valuable exercise in the course
- Compile your discussions into a **shared discipline-specific 4D document** with concrete examples
- Share this document with students to make AI Fluency concrete in your field
- Identify areas where colleagues *agree* and where they *differ* — both are valuable teaching material
- Create **decision frameworks** for when and how to involve AI in typical disciplinary workflows

## 💡 Top Practical Takeaways

1. **Choose your teaching approach deliberately.** Linear for beginners, loop-based for advanced students, focused when time is short. Match approach to student readiness.

2. **The two loops are nested, not sequential.** Teach Delegation-Diligence (strategy/ethics) and Description-Discernment (craft/iteration) as systems that contain and inform each other.

3. **Make the invisible visible.** Process-based assignments (annotated chat logs, recorded narrations) reveal the decision-making that outcome assessments miss. This is where real AI Fluency lives.

4. **Constraints are creative catalysts.** Ethical and accountability requirements (Diligence) don't limit Delegation — they *clarify and strengthen* AI collaboration choices. Teach this mindset explicitly.

5. **Create a reusable teaching context document.** Use Claude to synthesize your student backgrounds, goals, and constraints into a document you share at the start of new AI conversations. Saves time and improves consistency.

6. **Assess with all three lenses: outcome + process + reflection.** No single lens shows the full picture of AI Fluency. Combine strategically based on what's feasible for your course.

7. **Make tacit knowledge explicit with colleagues.** Your disciplinary expertise — quality standards, communication norms, ethical frameworks — is the foundation for meaningful AI integration. Make it explicit and shareable.

8. **Build discipline-specific answers to "Why learn X when AI can do it?"** Generic answers don't satisfy students. Ground your rationale in what's unique about human judgment in your field.

9. **Design for iteration, not one-shot prompts.** AI Fluency develops through sustained conversation and refinement — assignments should require multiple interactions over time.

10. **Peer review and selective sampling are your allies.** AI-enhanced work creates more student output. Use peer review for process components and selective sampling to keep grading manageable.
