## Course Overview

This course applies the **AI Fluency 4D Framework** specifically to teaching practice. It helps educators use AI as a genuine cognitive partner — not just an automation tool — for course design, material creation, and assessment development, while keeping pedagogical values at the center.

## Lesson Notes

### Lesson 1: Introduction to AI Fluency for Educators

**Key Concepts:**
- The **4D Framework** (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) applies directly to teaching practice
- AI Fluency for educators means **enhancing** rather than replacing human expertise and judgment
- **Context-building** is foundational — AI becomes far more helpful when it understands your specific situation, values, and constraints
- Three reasons AI Fluency in education matters *now*: students are already using AI; employers expect AI fluency; educators have the opportunity to model thoughtful engagement with these technologies
- The goal is to build a genuine **thinking partnership** with AI, grounded in pedagogical integrity

**Practical Tips:**
- Before engaging AI, clarify your own teaching values, constraints, and student profiles through self-reflection
- Create a **reusable Teaching Context Document** — have AI interview you about your teaching, then ask it to synthesize the conversation into a structured document you can paste into future AI sessions
- Cover these areas in your context document: subject area and courses, typical student challenges, institutional constraints, teaching philosophy, preferred methods, common pain points, and AI integration goals
- Encourage the AI to ask follow-up questions; share specific examples where helpful (mindful of privacy)
- Review the synthesized document together, correct it, and request a format that's easy to copy-paste

### Lesson 2: AI Fluency Framework Review

**Key Concepts:**
- **AI Fluency = Augmentation, not Automation** — working *with* AI to enhance your work is more powerful than having AI simply do work for you; this distinction is especially important in learning contexts
- The **4Ds** in brief:
  - **Delegation** — problem awareness, platform awareness, and task delegation (understanding *what* you need and *why* before deciding *how* to collaborate)
  - **Description** — communicating product, process, and performance aspects to the AI with sufficient context
  - **Discernment** — critical evaluation of AI outputs at all three levels; evaluating against your own expertise
  - **Diligence** — responsible, transparent, and accountable AI use
- **Description and Discernment form a continuous feedback loop** — this transforms AI from a tool into a thinking partner
- Each D has three sub-components addressing different aspects of AI interaction
- These skills remain relevant regardless of how AI technology evolves

**Practical Tips:**
- Think of Description + Discernment as an iterative refinement cycle, not a one-shot prompt
- If you've already taken the AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations course, this lesson can be skipped

### Lesson 3: Applying AI Fluency to Course Design and Learning Outcomes

**Key Concepts:**
- **Rich shared context transforms AI** from a generic assistant into a collaborative partner who understands your specific teaching situation
- Applying the 4Ds to course design covers three fundamental tasks: (1) identifying essential content and concepts, (2) mapping the learning journey, (3) articulating learning objectives
- **Delegation** in course design = understanding what you're trying to accomplish and why *before* deciding how to collaborate
- **Description** = inviting AI into your specific problem space with rich context, not just naming the course
- **Discernment** = evaluating suggestions against your expertise and *explaining back to the AI* why things work or don't for your students
- **Diligence** = maintaining responsibility, verifying accuracy, and documenting the collaborative process
- The goal is **enhancement, not efficiency** — better teaching and learning, not just faster planning

**Practical Tips:**
- Re-use your Teaching Context Document (from Lesson 1) as part of every new course design prompt
- Don't just name the course — explain specific challenges, what makes it unique (student needs, prerequisites), and what success looks like
- Ask AI to take on your students' perspective at transition points: *"What might confuse students here? What scaffolding is needed?"*
- For every AI suggestion, ask yourself: *Does this serve MY students?* Always explain your reasoning back to the AI
- Document key decisions and specifically note which AI suggestions you rejected and why
- Create a brief transparency statement about AI's role in your planning — consider sharing it with students or colleagues
- Check that learning objectives align with institutional requirements and ladder up to broader course goals

### Lesson 4: Applying AI Fluency to Learning Materials and Assignments

**Key Concepts:**
- **Established context makes each new workflow better** than starting from scratch — the more context you've built, the more coherent your materials become
- The **Description-Discernment loop** is the core refinement engine for material creation: not just accepting or rejecting AI suggestions, but explaining *why* they work or don't for your specific students
- Different materials require **different delegation strategies** (slides vs. quizzes vs. in-class exercises each have different dynamics)
- **Academic integrity** challenge: because students also have access to AI, educators should design assessments that promote authentic learning, not just AI-resistant tasks — AI fluency helps design better assessments
- The real transformation is in **quality**, not just speed — materials that build coherently, align with objectives, and embody your pedagogical vision
- **Diligence** in material creation includes: protecting sensitive data, verifying factual accuracy, checking for bias, and being transparent about AI's role

**Practical Tips:**
- When building slide decks, discuss what slides should accomplish *vs.* what you'll provide verbally — don't just ask for slides
- For study guides, ask AI to anticipate common student misconceptions and generate self-check questions at varying difficulty levels
- For in-class exercises, think through logistics with AI: group formation, pacing contingencies (*"What if this goes too fast? Too slow?"*), and facilitation notes
- For quizzes, ask AI to write both correct answer explanations *and* explanations of why wrong answers are wrong — this deepens student understanding
- Always verify factual accuracy, check for unintentional bias, and note where images or citations are needed before using AI-generated materials
- Evaluate coherence across materials: *"How did these materials build on and reference each other?"*

## Top 10 Practical Tips (Consolidated)

1. **Build a Teaching Context Document first.** Have AI interview you about your teaching practice, then save the synthesized result as a reusable artifact for every future AI session. This single investment pays dividends across all workflows.

2. **Clarify your own position before prompting.** Know your teaching values, student profiles, constraints, and goals *before* you engage AI — self-reflection up front makes your descriptions far richer.

3. **Always paste your Teaching Context Document into new sessions.** Don't let AI start from zero. Shared context is the foundation of genuine cognitive partnership.

4. **Treat Description + Discernment as a loop, not a one-shot.** Prompt → evaluate → explain your reasoning back → refine. Iteration is the mechanism, not a sign something went wrong.

5. **Explain *why* to the AI.** When you reject or modify a suggestion, tell the AI why it didn't fit your students or situation. This makes the next suggestion better and builds shared understanding.

6. **Ask AI to take your students' perspective.** At every design stage, prompt: *"What might confuse students here? What scaffolding is needed?"* This surfaces gaps you might overlook.

7. **Target enhancement, not just efficiency.** The goal is better teaching and learning outcomes, not faster output. Use saved time to improve quality, not just to do more.

8. **Document your AI collaboration process.** Note what AI contributed, what you modified, and what you rejected. This supports transparency with students and colleagues, and builds your own metacognitive awareness.

9. **Design for authentic learning, not AI-resistance.** Since students have AI access too, design assessments that require genuine understanding and application — AI fluency helps you think through what that actually means.

10. **Verify everything before use.** Factual accuracy, answer key correctness, potential bias, accessibility, and citation needs — always review AI-generated materials with your subject-matter expertise before they reach students.
